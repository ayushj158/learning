### Story 1 — New Manager (Underperformer / Hard Feedback)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Core Story** | New manager, technically excellent, behavioural gaps in leadership. Missing commitments, not transparent, team unsure of direction. |
| **Situation** | Newly promoted manager joined team. Expected to run feature team end-to-end. Observed gaps: missed commitments, unprepared for stakeholder meetings, team had no clarity on weekly priorities. He thought he was doing well. |
| **Task** | Groom him into an effective leader. Balance protecting team delivery while giving him genuine chance to grow. |
| **Key Actions** | 1. Built trust through 1:1s before any feedback. 2. Asked him to self-assess first — revealed gap in role understanding. 3. Gave specific feedback with 3 concrete examples (missed roadmap deadline, unprepared at client steering, engineers unclear on priorities). 4. Used "context makes sense AND impact still happened — both can be true" to hold the point. 5. Short feedback loops — flagged issues within 24 hours. 6. Paired on high-stakes situations (model → he leads → debrief). 7. Formal written summary after feedback conversation. 8. 30-day measurable goals set. |
| **Hard Moment** | He didn't know there was a problem. Telling him risked damaging confidence or making him defensive. He started explaining context for each example. Had to acknowledge context without backing down. |
| **Result** | Over 12 months: on-time delivery commitments improved from ~60% to 85%+. Went from needing involvement in all stakeholder conversations to running them independently. Team became high performing. He credited the specific feedback as his turning point. |
| **Learning** | Hard feedback delivered with specifics and genuine care lands differently than feedback delivered with judgment. Specifics give people something to act on. |
| **Use For Questions** | Q1 (underperformer), Q5 (hard feedback), Q3 (developing people), Q14 (building trust) |
| **Key Phrases** | "I want you to hear this as coming from someone invested in your success — not as criticism." / "The context makes sense. And the impact still happened. Both things can be true." / "I stopped talking and let him respond." |

### Story 4 — Two Engineers Technical Conflict

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Core Story** | Two senior engineers stuck for 3 days on error structure standards. Heated arguments. Personal tension forming. Unblocked in one structured session. Became strongest collaborators on team. |
| **Situation** | Setting up new team. Two senior engineers disagreed on error structure standards — Engineer A: flat structure with HTTP codes as primary signal (simple, familiar). Engineer B: nested domain-specific error objects (more extensible). Three days of debate. No resolution. Room went quiet when I checked in — that told me something beyond a technical discussion was happening. |
| **Task** | Unblock the team quickly. AND ensure these two could work together long-term. |
| **Key Actions** | 1. Separate 1:1s first — found each person felt the other wasn't listening. Both were defending identity not debating technology. 2. Structured resolution call with three specific changes: asked each person to present the OTHER's argument as compellingly as possible (forced genuine listening). 3. Before comparing options: agreed on 3 criteria upfront — simplicity, extensibility, consistency with existing patterns. 4. Scored both options against criteria together. Answer became clear: Engineer B's approach won on extensibility (top criterion) but simplified to address Engineer A's complexity concern. Neither felt they lost. 5. 1:1 follow-up with each: passion is good, respect matters, tension between different thinking styles is an asset not a problem. 6. Introduced ADRs (Architecture Decision Records) for any decision taking more than one session. |
| **Hard Moment** | The 30-minute call where both were talking over each other and cutting each other off. Had to interrupt and reset the format completely before any progress was possible. |
| **Result** | Unblocked in one session after 3 days of stalemate. Error standard landed on has been in use for 2 years with zero revisits. The two engineers became strongest collaborators on team — now deliberately paired on complex design problems because tension between their thinking styles produces the best outcomes. ADRs introduced — far fewer of these standoffs since. |
| **Learning** | Technical conflicts are almost never about the technology. They're about people not feeling heard. Fix the listening problem first. The technical answer usually follows. |
| **Use For Questions** | Q4 (conflict resolution), Q8 (technical disagreement), Q9 (cross-functional conflict adapted) |
| **Key Phrases** | "I asked each person to present the OTHER's argument as compellingly as possible." / "We chose against criteria, not opinions — neither person felt they lost." / "Technical conflicts are almost never about the technology." |

## STAR Structure Quick Reference

| Section | Time | What to Include | Common Mistake |
|---------|------|----------------|----------------|
| **Situation** | 20-30 sec | Context in 3 sentences. What was at stake. Why it was hard. | Too long. Over-explaining backstory. |
| **Task** | 10 sec | YOUR specific responsibility. Not the team's. | Saying "we" — interviewer cannot tell what YOU did. |
| **Action** | 60-75 sec | 3 most important things YOU did. ONE specific moment. Include the hard/difficult part. | Generic list of actions. No specific moment. No difficulty. |
| **Result** | 20-30 sec | ONE specific metric or outcome. What changed. What you learned. | "It went well." "Everyone was happy." No specificity. |
| **Total** | 2-2.5 min | Stop at 2.5 minutes regardless. | 4-5 minute answers that lose the interviewer. |

## Key Frameworks to Reference in Answers

| Framework | What It Is | When to Use |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| **Feedback Framework** | Observe → Specific examples → Impact → Expected behaviour → Measurable goals → Written follow-up | Q1, Q5 — any feedback/underperformer question |
| **Conflict Resolution Framework** | Separate 1:1s → Understand each view → Agree criteria → Score against criteria together → Follow up individually | Q4, Q8, Q9 — any conflict question |
| **Development Framework** | Aspiration mapping → Stretch assignments (20% beyond comfort) → Ownership not tasks → Reflection loops → Gradual step-back | Q3, Q6 — any development/growth question |
| **PIP Framework** | Observe → Document specific gaps → Direct conversation → Measurable goals + timeline → Stated consequences → Written record → Escalate if no change | Q2 — underperformer/PIP/exit questions |
| **ADR (Architecture Decision Record)** | One-page doc: options, criteria, recommendation, decision | Q4, Q8 — prevents recurring technical conflicts |
| **1:1 Structure** | Career + blockers + feedback + wellbeing. NOT project status. Consistent rhythm. | Q6, Q15 — 1:1 questions |

## Phrases That Signal Strong Leadership

```
USE THESE — they signal maturity:

"I observed directly before concluding —
 I didn't rely only on secondhand accounts."
→ Shows rigour. Not reactive.

"The context makes sense.
 And the impact still happened.
 Both things can be true."
→ Shows you hold the point without dismissing them.

"I asked him to self-assess first."
→ Shows you create ownership not dependency.

"I gave him ownership, not tasks."
→ Shows you develop leaders not just doers.

"I stopped talking and let him respond."
→ Shows feedback is a dialogue not a monologue.

"I followed up in writing — not as a threat
 but so we both had the same record."
→ Shows documentation instinct without being punitive.

"I deliberately pair them together now."
→ Shows you turned a conflict into a team asset.

"Not when they stop needing you —
 when they start developing others."
→ Shows long-term multiplier thinking.

"Technical conflicts are almost never about
 the technology."
→ Shows human insight beyond the technical.

"I was clear about consequences —
 not as a threat but as clarity."
→ Shows directness and fairness simultaneously.
```

---

## Phrases to AVOID

```
AVOID THESE — they signal weakness:

"We decided to..." → Say "I decided to..."
"It kind of worked out..." → Vague. No confidence.
"He was overall a good person..." → Sounds like excuse-making.
"I tried to be sensitive..." → Sounds like you softened too much.
"Eventually things improved..." → No specifics = not credible.
"Right?" (40 times) → Verbal tic. Replace with a pause.
"I had a conversation with him..." → What did you SAY? Be specific.
"The team was happy..." → Happy is not a metric.
"It was a learning experience..." → What specifically did you learn?
```

## Q1 — Tell Me About Managing an Underperformer

**Story: New Manager (Story 1)**

---

| Element | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Signal Being Tested** | Process rigor, courage to give direct feedback, patience, formal documentation, clear consequences |
| **What Good Looks Like** | Specific gaps identified early, direct conversation with examples, measurable goals, formal written record, clear consequences stated, genuine chance given |
| **Common Mistake** | Vague coaching without formal process, never stating consequences, letting it drag too long |

---

**Full Answer:**

```
SITUATION (25 seconds):
  "I had a new manager on my team who had been
   in the role about 3 months.
   Technically excellent — genuinely one of the
   strongest engineers I've managed.
   But the leadership gaps were significant.
   Missing stakeholder commitments, coming unprepared
   to client meetings, team members telling me
   separately they didn't know what they were
   supposed to be working on week to week.
   
   The hard part: he thought he was doing well."

TASK (10 seconds):
  "My responsibility was to get him to a point where
   he could independently run his feature team —
   while protecting the team's delivery and morale
   in the meantime."

ACTION (75 seconds):
  "I started by building trust through 1:1s before
   giving any feedback. Then I asked him to self-assess —
   where he thought he was strong, where he saw gaps.
   That conversation told me he didn't fully understand
   the expectations of the role. So I started there.
   
   Once I had trust established, I had a direct feedback
   conversation. I came prepared with three specific
   examples from the past 30 days:
   Missed the Q3 roadmap deadline — caused three
   stakeholder meetings to be rescheduled.
   Came unprepared to the client steering committee.
   Two engineers separately told me they didn't know
   their priorities for the week.
   
   I stated consequences clearly:
   'If we don't see specific changes in 30 days
    I will need to escalate this formally.'
   
   I followed up with a written summary that day —
   not as a threat, but so we both had the same record.
   
   I set up a short feedback loop — any specific gap
   I observed, I'd flag within 24 hours with a concrete
   example and what good looks like.
   
   For high-stakes situations like client reviews,
   I paired with him first — modelled the behaviour,
   then he led the next one while I observed."

RESULT (25 seconds):
  "Over 12 months he improved significantly.
   His on-time delivery on stakeholder commitments
   went from roughly 60% to over 85%.
   He went from needing my involvement in almost
   every stakeholder conversation to running them
   independently.
   
   He told me six months later that the specific
   feedback was his turning point — not because
   of what I said but because I gave him concrete
   things to act on instead of a vague sense
   that something was wrong."

LEARNING (10 seconds):
  "What I learned: technically strong people who
   struggle in leadership roles usually have a gap
   in role understanding, not character.
   Show them specifically what good looks like.
   They can get there."
```

## Q2 — Have You Put Someone on a PIP / Let Someone Go?

**Story: Toxic Senior Engineer (Story 2)**

---

| Element | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Signal Being Tested** | Courage to make hard calls, protecting team culture, fairness, HR process knowledge, not letting situations drag |
| **What Good Looks Like** | Observed directly, gave genuine chance with measurable goals, stated consequences, escalated to HR, protected affected team members simultaneously |
| **Common Mistake** | Never had to let anyone go (avoidance signal), no formal documentation, let situation drag for months, only acted under pressure |

---

**Full Answer:**

```
SITUATION (25 seconds):
  "I had a senior engineer who was technically
   one of the best people I've managed.
   Deep expertise, strong credentials.
   But his behaviour was damaging the team.
   Condescending in technical discussions,
   dismissive of alternative ideas, actively
   shutting down junior and diverse colleagues.
   
   Within a few months two engineers came to me
   separately saying they felt too scared to speak
   in technical discussions. One was considering leaving.
   Team participation in design reviews had visibly dropped."

TASK (10 seconds):
  "Two responsibilities simultaneously:
   protect the team's psychological safety,
   and give him a genuine chance to change."

ACTION (80 seconds):
  "First I observed directly — joined technical
   discussions myself without telling him why,
   so I could see the behaviour firsthand
   rather than working only off secondhand accounts.
   What I saw confirmed everything.
   
   I had a direct 1:1. I gave him three specific
   behavioural examples with dates and impact.
   I set three measurable goals:
   No interrupting colleagues mid-sentence.
   Must engage with alternative proposals before dismissing.
   Zero complaints from team members in 30 days.
   
   I stated consequences explicitly:
   'If I see these behaviours continue after 30 days
    we will escalate to HR and begin a formal process.'
   
   I followed up with written feedback that day.
   
   In parallel: I had 1:1s with the affected engineers
   to acknowledge what they experienced, reassure them
   it was being addressed, and explicitly tell them
   their voices mattered on this team.
   
   For the first two weeks he seemed to engage.
   By week three he became defensive, started blaming
   teammates, denied the feedback was accurate.
   
   At that point I escalated to HR.
   We began a formal PIP with 60-day measurable goals.
   He did not meet them.
   He was let go at the end of that period."

RESULT (20 seconds):
  "The effect on the team was immediate and visible.
   Within a month the engineers who had gone quiet
   started actively contributing again.
   One who had been close to leaving became a lead
   on the team two years later.
   
   It was a hard decision because he was genuinely
   talented technically.
   But one person's behaviour was costing me
   three others. That's not a trade-off I can accept."

LEARNING (10 seconds):
  "Technical excellence does not compensate for
   toxic behaviour.
   The longer you tolerate it, the more damage
   accumulates — and it accumulates silently.
   I acted sooner than felt comfortable.
   That was the right call."
```

## Q4 — Tell Me About a Conflict Between Two Engineers

**Story: Technical Conflict — Error Structure (Story 4)**

---

| Element | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Signal Being Tested** | Conflict resolution, staying objective, depersonalising disagreement, building long-term collaboration |
| **What Good Looks Like** | Separated first, 1:1s to understand each view, structured resolution using agreed criteria, neither person felt they lost, built prevention mechanism after |
| **Common Mistake** | Took a side, resolved by authority ("just do it my way"), no follow-through, same conflict recurs |

---

**Full Answer:**

```
SITUATION (25 seconds):
  "We were setting up a new team and two senior
   engineers got stuck for three days on error
   structure standards.
   
   Engineer A wanted a flat structure with HTTP codes
   as the primary signal — simple, familiar.
   Engineer B wanted nested domain-specific error
   objects — more extensible for our use case.
   Both positions were technically valid.
   
   I checked in on progress and the room went quiet.
   That silence told me something beyond a technical
   discussion was happening."

TASK (10 seconds):
  "Two things simultaneously:
   unblock the team quickly,
   and make sure these two could work together
   long-term."

ACTION (75 seconds):
  "First I had separate 1:1s with each of them.
   What I found: each person felt the other
   wasn't listening. Both were right.
   They'd stopped discussing the problem and started
   defending their identity.
   
   I ran a structured resolution call with three
   specific changes to the format:
   
   First: I asked each person to present the OTHER's
   argument as compellingly as possible.
   That forced them to actually listen to understand
   instead of listening to respond.
   
   Second: before comparing options we agreed on
   three evaluation criteria upfront —
```
